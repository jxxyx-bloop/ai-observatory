# The Southeast Asia and China product thesis

Why a token tracker built for this user base is a different product, not a
translated one.

## The user, specifically

Not "a developer." Four of them, and the design serves them in this order:

1. **The plan-constrained solo dev.** Jakarta, Ho Chi Minh City, Manila, Chengdu.
   Pays $10–30/month for one coding plan, sometimes shares it. Hits quota walls
   mid-task and has no idea why. Cares about *headroom*, not spend.
2. **The multi-vendor pragmatist.** Runs Claude Code for hard reasoning, GLM or
   DeepSeek for bulk work, Qwen for anything with Chinese in it. Has three
   subscriptions and no idea which is carrying its weight. This person is
   *invisible* to every vendor dashboard, because no vendor sees the other two.
3. **The small agency / dev shop.** 3–20 people, Vietnam, Philippines, Malaysia,
   Shenzhen. Bills clients by project. Genuinely needs "which client is burning
   my seats" — and genuinely must not become a surveillance tool while answering
   it.
4. **The enterprise adopter under a compliance regime.** Needs to demonstrate
   that a tool touching developer telemetry stores no code and transfers no
   personal data across a border it should not cross.

## The eight structural differences

### 1. The unit of purchase is a plan, not a token

Coding plans dominate: GLM Coding Lite at $18/month, Pro at $80, Max at $168;
Qwen's ModelStudio Coding Plan around $50; Claude Pro at $20. In a market where
a senior contract rate is $85–130/day, a $200/month Max seat is a serious line
item and a $18 plan is the normal case.

**Consequence.** The dollar estimate every competitor headlines is a shadow
price here. The real metrics are *plan-value multiple* (what the plan returned)
and *quota headroom* (how much of the week is left). We compute both;
nobody else computes either.

### 2. Quotas are denominated in units that are not tokens

GLM meters **credits** — 10,000/week on Lite, 6× that on Pro — *and* imposes a
prompts-per-5-hours cap on top. Claude meters prompts on a rolling 5-hour window
and a weekly one. Copilot meters premium requests per month. These are different
units with different reset clocks, and several vendors publish no number at all.

**Consequence.** "Quota headroom" cannot be one formula. `plans.json` models
unit, window kind, window length and amount separately, and where a vendor
publishes nothing we fall back to a self-calibrated p90 of the user's own
history — the trick
[Claude-Code-Usage-Monitor](https://github.com/Maciek-roboblog/Claude-Code-Usage-Monitor)
got right and is worth copying outright.

### 3. Tokens are priced by the hour

DeepSeek moved V4 to peak/off-peak billing on 2026-08-16: peak 01:00–04:00 and
06:00–10:00 UTC, everything else at half. Z.ai's GLM peaks 14:00–18:00 UTC+8 on
weekdays only, so weekends are entirely off-peak.

**Consequence, and it is a big one.** For a developer in UTC+7 or +8, ordinary
working hours land squarely inside DeepSeek's second peak window (06:00–10:00
UTC is 13:00–17:00 in Jakarta, 14:00–18:00 in Shanghai). They are paying peak
rates by accident, all afternoon, every day. Nothing in the interactive part of
their work can move — but test generation, migrations, doc sweeps and bulk
refactors can, at no cost to them. This is a real, quantified, regionally
specific saving that no other tool can even see, and it is implemented in
[`pricing.py`](../../observatory/pricing.py) with the counterfactual carried
through the fact cube.

### 4. The model landscape is genuinely different

Lingma (通义灵码) has 10M+ IDE plugin installs. Baidu reports 43% of its internal
code generated by Comate with ~90% of its programmers using it. Qwen Code claims
5M+ users. Kimi Code targets CLI-and-DevOps workflows. Meanwhile Qwen Code's
free OAuth tier retired in April 2026 and iFlow CLI sunset the same month —
churn that strands users mid-workflow.

**Consequence.** Tool coverage cannot wait on a maintainer who owns none of
these tools. That is the entire reason for the declarative spec collector: the
person who uses Lingma is the only person who can add Lingma correctly, and
their contribution must cost one JSON file plus a fixture, not a Python module
plus a review cycle. Coverage breadth is the strongest correlate with stars in
this category, and this is the only way to get it in a market this fragmented.

### 5. Cache discounts are not standardised

The 0.1× cache-hit multiplier is Anthropic's convention. Moonshot's Kimi K2.6
holds a cache-hit floor of $0.07/M against a $0.95/M input rate — about 0.074×.
GLM and DeepSeek differ again.

**Consequence.** Cache reuse is where most of the money is, so a wrong
multiplier misprices the single most important number on the page. Every model
in `pricing.json` can carry its own `cache_read_mult`, and the test suite asserts
the Kimi case specifically.

### 6. Money is thought about in local currency, and against local rates

`$412/month` means something different in Jakarta than in San Francisco.
token-monitor already does TWD/HKD/CNY conversion — correctly reading the
demand — but stops at conversion.

**Consequence.** We convert to thirteen currencies including IDR, VND, THB, PHP
and MYR, and go one step further: *"that is roughly 4.6 days of a median local
contract rate."* Converting a number is a feature. Framing it against local
opportunity cost is an argument, and arguments are what get shared.

### 7. Data-protection law is a live constraint, not a footnote

Indonesia's PDP Law requires localisation for some categories and permits
cross-border transfer only on specific grounds. Vietnam's PDPL took effect
1 January 2026 with restrictions on transferring "core" or "important" data.
Thailand's PDPA has designated no adequate jurisdictions at all, so transfers
run on standard contractual clauses or explicit consent with risk disclosure.
Malaysia removed its whitelist in favour of an equivalence test. China's PIPL
requires CAC-approved standard contracts for certain transfers.

**Consequence.** A community layer that ships raw per-user rows to a US server
is not a privacy preference in this market — it is a legal problem for the
user's employer. Ours is designed so that the question mostly does not arise:
metrics are bucketed **on the device**, the payload is under a kilobyte and
carries no identifier, no repo name and no raw count, and the self-host path is
a first-class option rather than an enterprise upsell.

### 8. Community happens in different places, in different languages

WeChat groups, Xiaohongshu, Juejin, V2EX, Zhihu and Bilibili in China; Telegram,
Discord, Facebook groups and local-language dev communities across SEA. English
is a second language for most of this audience.

**Consequence.** Bilingual EN/ZH from the first commit, not as a later
translation pass — the source repo this was extracted from already shipped a
full EN/ZH string table, so we start with the hard part done. Shareable
artefacts must be *images*, because that is what travels on Xiaohongshu and
WeChat, where a GitHub link does not.

## What we deliberately do *not* assume

Being specific about a region is useful; being wrong about it is not.

- **Not "cheap models only."** The multi-vendor pragmatist pays for Claude
  precisely because it is worth it on hard turns. The product must never imply
  that switching to the cheapest model is the answer — the finding is *route
  mechanical work down a tier*, not *stop using good models*.
- **Not "everyone is price-sensitive."** Singapore and Hong Kong rates are close
  to Western ones. Currency and plan lenses are configurable, and USD/no-plan
  remains the honest default.
- **Not "China is one market."** Access, payment rails and vendor availability
  differ sharply between the mainland, Hong Kong, Taiwan and the diaspora. We
  model *vendors*, never geography, and the only place geography appears is a
  self-declared cohort label the user types themselves.
- **Not "Chinese models are the story."** They are one input. A Filipino
  developer on Copilot Pro is as much the user as a Shenzhen developer on GLM.

## The defensibility question

Any of this can be copied. The question is whether it *will* be.

The regional model is the kind of work that is only obvious from inside the
market: you have to have been surprised by a peak-rate bill, or have counted
credits against a weekly reset, to know these are the metrics. A maintainer in
San Francisco optimising for the American developer will not prioritise
thirteen currencies, two pricing clocks, and a PDPA-shaped consent model —
not because they cannot, but because their users are not asking for it.

That is a real head start. It is not a permanent moat, and the durable part is
the second-order asset: **a community-maintained rate card, plan table and
collector-spec library**, contributed by the people who actually buy from these
vendors. That compounds, and it is expensive to replicate because it is made of
other people's attention.
